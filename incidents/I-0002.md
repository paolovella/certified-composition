# Stopping a benchmark killed the wrapper that was recording it

**What happened.** The S-KERNEL sweep (`tools/run-job.sh skernel-kernel -- python3
bench/kernel/run.py ...`) was stopped on purpose once its result was clear (see
`backlog/S-KERNEL.md`). It was stopped with `pkill -f "bench/kernel/run.py"`. That pattern also
matched the command line of the `run-job.sh` wrapper, whose arguments contain the same path,
and of the shell that ran pkill. The wrapper died, so `skernel-kernel.end` was never written.
The `lean` child kept running as an orphan until it was found and killed by hand.

**Why it mattered.** A receipt is meant to be written whatever happens to the job. Here the
wrapper was killed while it waited on a foreground child, and bash defers a trap until a
foreground child exits, so it could not record its own end. The only evidence was the
open-job line in `STATE.md`. That is the backstop working, but it is not a record of what
happened.

**What would have caught it.** `run-job.sh` now runs the job in the background and `wait`s for
it, which a trap can interrupt. On TERM, INT or HUP it passes the signal to the job and writes
`exit: signal NAME`. `check-claims` (f) starts a job, sends it TERM, and requires that receipt;
its accepted witness is a completed job recording `exit: 0`. SIGKILL still leaves no `.end`, and
`render-state.sh` reports that case.

**Recovery.** `skernel-kernel.end` was written by hand with `exit: killed` and a note saying it
was written by hand. The rows measured before the stop are kept as run 1 in `bench/kernel.tsv`.

**Operator rule.** Stop a job by its PID (`pgrep -f` first, read the list, then `kill PID`),
never with a `pkill -f` pattern that can match the wrapper or your own shell.
